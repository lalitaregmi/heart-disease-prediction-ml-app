Heart Disease Risk Factors

Understanding what increases your risk is the first step toward prevention.

What Are Risk Factors?

Risk factors are conditions or behaviors that increase your chance of developing heart disease. Some risk factors cannot be changed, such as age and family history, but many others can be modified through lifestyle changes and medical interventions.

The more risk factors you have, the greater your chance of developing heart disease. However, having one or more risk factors doesn't mean you will definitely develop heart disease, and not having any risk factors doesn't guarantee you won't.

Understanding your personal risk factors is crucial for developing an effective prevention strategy. Our risk assessment tool can help you identify your specific risk profile.

Non-modifiable (cannot be changed)
Lifestyle (can be modified)
Medical conditions (can be managed)

Non-modifiable Risk Factors

Age

The risk of heart disease increases as you get older. Men over 45 and women over 55 (or after menopause) are at higher risk.

Why it matters: Aging causes changes to the heart and blood vessels, including arterial stiffening and decreased elasticity.

Sex

Men generally have a higher risk of heart disease than pre-menopausal women. After menopause, women's risk increases to nearly that of men.

Why it matters: Estrogen provides some protection against heart disease before menopause. Men tend to develop heart disease about 10 years earlier than women.

Family History

Your risk increases if a first-degree relative (parent or sibling) had heart disease at an early age (before 55 for men or 65 for women).

Why it matters: Genetic factors can influence heart structure, blood vessel function, and how your body processes cholesterol and other substances.

Race and Ethnicity

Some racial and ethnic groups have higher risks of heart disease. African Americans, for example, have higher rates of hypertension, a major risk factor.

Why it matters: Differences may be due to genetic factors, higher rates of certain conditions, and social determinants of health including access to healthcare.

Previous Heart Events

If you've already had a heart attack, stroke, or other heart-related event, your risk of having another one is significantly higher.

Why it matters: Previous events indicate existing cardiovascular damage and underlying conditions that increase vulnerability to future events.

Menopause

Women's risk of heart disease increases after menopause due to decreasing estrogen levels, which have protective effects on the heart.

Why it matters: Estrogen helps keep blood vessels flexible and helps maintain healthy levels of HDL (good) cholesterol. These benefits diminish after menopause.

Modifiable Risk Factors

Major Modifiable Risk Factors

These factors have the strongest evidence for increasing heart disease risk and should be primary targets for prevention.

Smoking

Smoking damages blood vessels, reduces oxygen in the blood, and increases blood pressure and heart rate. It doubles or even quadruples your risk of heart disease.

Risk reduction: Quitting smoking can reduce your risk by up to 50% after one year, and approach that of a non-smoker after 15 years.

High Blood Pressure

High blood pressure (hypertension) forces your heart to work harder to pump blood, damaging arteries and increasing the risk of heart attack, stroke, and heart failure.

Risk reduction: Lowering blood pressure through lifestyle changes and/or medication can significantly reduce your risk of heart disease and stroke.

High Cholesterol

High levels of LDL ("bad") cholesterol and low levels of HDL ("good") cholesterol increase the risk of plaque buildup in your arteries, leading to atherosclerosis.

Risk reduction: A 10% reduction in total cholesterol levels can reduce heart disease risk by as much as 30%.

Physical Inactivity

Regular physical activity strengthens your heart and improves circulation. Lack of exercise is linked to 6% of heart disease cases worldwide.

Obesity

Excess weight, especially around the waist, increases the strain on your heart and contributes to high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and diabetes.

Diabetes

High blood sugar damages blood vessels and nerves that control your heart. Adults with diabetes are 2-4 times more likely to die from heart disease.

Poor Diet

Diets high in saturated fats, trans fats, salt, and sugar increase risk factors like cholesterol, blood pressure, and obesity.

Excessive Alcohol

Heavy drinking can raise blood pressure, increase certain types of cholesterol, and contribute to heart muscle damage (cardiomyopathy).

Stress

Chronic stress may increase inflammation in your body, raise blood pressure, and lead to unhealthy coping behaviors like overeating or smoking.

Sleep Apnea

This sleep disorder causes breathing to stop and start repeatedly during sleep, reducing oxygen levels and increasing heart strain.

Poor Sleep

Regularly sleeping less than 7 hours per night is associated with higher risk of heart disease, regardless of other factors like age or weight.

The Multiplier Effect

Risk factors don't exist in isolation. When multiple risk factors are present, they can interact and multiply your overall risk of heart disease, not just add to it.

Risk Factor Combinations

Consider these examples of how risk factors can interact:

  • Smoking + High Blood Pressure: These two factors together increase heart disease risk more than the sum of their individual risks.
  • Diabetes + High Cholesterol: This combination accelerates damage to blood vessels and increases heart attack risk substantially.
  • Obesity + Physical Inactivity: Together these create a cycle that worsens insulin resistance and inflammation.

Metabolic Syndrome

Metabolic syndrome is a cluster of conditions that occur together, significantly increasing your risk of heart disease. You have metabolic syndrome if you have three or more of these conditions:

  • Large waist circumference (≥35" for women, ≥40" for men)
  • High triglyceride level (≥150 mg/dL)
  • Low HDL cholesterol (<40 mg/dL in men, <50 mg/dL in women)
  • High blood pressure (≥130/85 mm Hg)
  • High fasting blood sugar (≥100 mg/dL)

Important Note

The good news is that addressing even one risk factor can have positive effects on others. For example, increasing physical activity can help with weight management, lower blood pressure, improve cholesterol levels, and reduce stress.
